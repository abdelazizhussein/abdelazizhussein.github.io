Science History Book Recommendations

Jan 1, 2026 — Physics ▶

More often than not, physics is taught as a collection of equations to memorize and plug numbers into. That picture could not be further from the truth. Science is done by humans (at least until recently; with the advent of AI the conversation gets much more complicated, and since many people are already chiming in, I will stay out of it here. See David Hogg's take (arXiv:2602.10181) for a great discussion). The train of thought behind a result, which can take decades and many failed attempts, is as important to learn as the material itself if you intend to pursue physics as a career.

Below is a non-comprehensive list of books that I really enjoyed and that helped me put some of the physics I learned, and the physicists who came up with it, in context.

Quantum: Einstein, Bohr and the Great Debate About the Nature of Reality

Manjit Kumar

The Strangest Man: The Hidden Life of Paul Dirac, Mystic of the Atom

Graham Farmelo

A biography of Dirac. It is excellent until the final chapters, where some of the claims about Dirac giving birth to string theory are too speculative.

Niels Bohr's Times, In Physics, Philosophy, and Polity

Abraham Pais

Incomplete and Incorrect Theories Should Be Encouraged

Nov 30, 2025 — Outreach ▶

One thing that truly bothers me is how physics classes often move at a pace that strips away the years (or even decades) it took to develop not only a theory but also the intuition behind it. Below are two examples I find particularly striking.

Heisenberg, beta decay and the strong force

One of the most interesting examples, and one rarely discussed in undergraduate classes, is the history of the strong force. I stumbled upon it while reading the Wikipedia page on the Yukawa potential and its origins. Physicists wrestled with why the nucleus doesn't blow itself apart under the repulsion between its protons, and with whether the neutron is a composite particle (made up of an electron and a proton). Heisenberg, best known for his uncertainty principle, played a key role in refining our understanding of the strong force and in part inspired the work of Yukawa and later Fermi. Though his theory was incomplete and incorrect, it contained remarkable insights, such as isospin, and eventually paved the way to a coherent theory of the strong force. I truly see it as a necessary step in that development, and I think students should be encouraged to see incomplete and incorrect theories the same way: they are often a necessary step toward a coherent theory.

Abraham and the theory of the electron

Another interesting example is the theory of the electron. Before Einstein’s special relativity explained the electrodynamics of moving bodies, Lorentz, Poincaré and Abraham were developing their own theories of the electron. You have probably heard of the first two, but Max Abraham may have fallen through the cracks. Abraham was Planck’s student, and he wrote THE book on Maxwell’s theory of electromagnetism in Germany.

At the time, new experimental results by W. Kaufmann were showing that the mass of the electron depends on its velocity. This was one of the defining moments when one had to reconcile Newton’s dynamics with Maxwell’s electromagnetism, and it was an opportunity to unify both into a “theory of everything” of sorts. Perhaps it is a good reminder that attempts at unified theories are not superfluous and can, historically, provide great physical insight.

To really tackle this issue, one had to confront the idea of the aether, whether the electron is a rigid sphere, and how charge is distributed over its shape. Though Einstein’s theory prevailed, I believe this story reminds us of a time when the theories being built truly challenged our world view, and one had to venture into the unknown with only an intuition for how the universe might behave. Perhaps the lesson from Abraham is not to be stubborn, but it is equally important not to be afraid to take a stab at describing how nature works.

Today, if you ask a graduate student about the theory of relativity, they will quickly recite the axioms (the speed of light is constant, …) and jump to consequences such as time dilation and length contraction, because these are very flashy results. However, it all began with the humble electron (that is why Einstein’s 1905 paper is titled On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies), and I believe this is an excellent example of what professors mean when they say that asking the right question matters.
